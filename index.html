Recette - film photo editor

react native · expo · skia shaders - solo-designed, closed beta
screenshot / demo video

What if the edit was the thing you shared, not just the photo?

I'm a photography amateur, and I've always admired edits that make a picture feel like a still from a movie. But color grading is genuinely hard to learn - and even once you know the tools, it's weirdly difficult to land the same feel as an image you're chasing. There's this big gap between admiring someone's photo and being able to reproduce it yourself.

So I built Recette: a social platform where you edit your photos and post them - but every edit is a copyable recipe. You can browse scene styles, find a look you love, and reuse someone else's effect on your own shot. The whole loop is designed to shrink that distance between "I wish mine looked like that" and actually getting there.

The central bet was treating the recipe as a first-class object rather than a hidden byproduct. Most editing apps keep the "how" locked inside the app or the person; making it the thing people share and remix is what turns a tool into a social loop. That one decision shaped everything downstream - the feed, the profile, the editor all had to make recipes legible and reusable, not just the images.

A couple of the trickier calls came from that. I took inspiration from VSCO's locked-layer system so recipes stay structured and reproducible instead of collapsing into one flat filter - you can see and adjust the steps, not just the result. And I leaned on Skia shaders to run the edits on-device, so the pipeline feels immediate rather than round-tripping to a server for every tweak.

It's in closed beta now, and I'm testing a creator incentive system to see whether the recipe-sharing loop actually drives engagement - i.e. whether people share recipes because it feels good, or whether it needs a nudge. That's the open question I'm validating next.

Reputation over time - interactive research map

python · leaflet · selenium · roberta - group project, with school's social sciences team
screenshot / demo video

How do you see a reputation change after something happens?

This came from the school's social sciences team, who had a genuinely open research question: how do social events influence the reputation of a place over time? That's the kind of question that's easy to ask and hard to actually study - reputation is fuzzy, it's spread across thousands of scattered opinions, and it moves. They needed a way to see it, not just describe it.

So we built an interactive map that turns scattered public reviews into something you can read geographically and temporally. We scraped Google reviews, ran sentiment analysis on them with a RoBERTa model to turn free text into a signal you can actually track, and plotted it on a Leaflet map that shows how sentiment around a place shifts over time - so an event and its reputational aftermath become visible as a pattern, not a spreadsheet.

The core design problem was legibility: temporal + geographic + sentiment is three dimensions at once, and the easy version is an unreadable mess. The decision that mattered was choosing what to not show - resisting the urge to surface every data point and instead designing for the one thing the researchers actually needed to perceive: change, over time, in a place. That restraint is what made it a research instrument rather than a data dump.

Being a group project with an external stakeholder, my focus was the UI/UX design. The interesting part wasn't the scraping or the model - it was working backward from a vague human question to a tool that made the answer visible.

Voice-driven VR architecture studio

unity · meta quest · gemini api - group project
screenshot / demo video

Designing a building shouldn't require learning to be a gamer first.

This started from a conversation with a PhD student from an architecture background, who described how genuinely slow it is to build architectural prototypes and iterate on them. That stuck with me - the bottleneck wasn't ideas, it was the friction of getting them into a workable form. I wanted a tool that made the whole loop feel intuitive and fast.

So we built a VR studio where architects can shape and iterate on prototypes in space. The core move was swapping controllers for voice input. Handing a non-gamer architect two Quest controllers and expecting fluent 3D manipulation is a big ask - the controls become the barrier. Voice sidesteps that: you describe what you want, and the friction of the interface mostly disappears. That let the tool meet its actual users where they are, rather than where a gamer would be.

That decision drove the harder problems too. Voice input meant leaning on the Gemini API to turn loose natural language into structured actions - which is a very different design constraint from mapping a button to a function. You're designing for ambiguity and intent, not precise input, so a lot of the work was making the system forgiving of how people actually phrase things.

Being a group project, my focus was on designing intuitive interfaces. We ran 7 design revisions with 9 users before shipping to headset - that iteration count is the part I'm most happy with, because most of the real learning was in watching people fumble a command we thought was obvious and reworking it.

Accessibility-focused sports vision

python · opencv · yolov9 · arduino - top 3% nationally, graded 20/20
screenshot / demo video

Can a blind spectator follow a football match in real time?

The 2024 national engineering entrance exam research project had the theme "sports," which is wide open - most people went toward performance or analytics. I went the other way and asked who can't currently enjoy a match: visually-impaired spectators, who get sound from the stadium but lose the thread of where the ball actually is. That felt like the more interesting problem, and a more human one.

So I built an assistive tracking system that follows the game and conveys ball position to a spectator who can't see it - AI ball recognition plus terrain detection to know where on the pitch the action is, translated into something you can feel rather than see.

The decision that shaped everything was optimizing for real-time over precision. A match doesn't wait - a tracking system that's accurate but lags is useless, because the spectator would be feeling a play that already happened. So I designed around latency as the hard constraint and accepted a tolerable margin of error in exchange: 180ms inference, with ~6m localization error. That ~6m isn't a shortcoming I'm hiding - it's a deliberate trade, precise enough to convey "the ball is on the left wing near the box" while staying fast enough to feel live.

That framing drove the physical side too. I evaluated 60 camera placements to find the accuracy/coverage sweet spot - where a camera sees enough of the pitch without degrading tracking - which is the kind of tradeoff you only find by testing systematically rather than guessing.

The project placed in the top 3% nationally and was graded 20/20 - but the part I care about is that it started from "who's being left out?" rather than "what's technically cool," which is still how I like to pick problems.
